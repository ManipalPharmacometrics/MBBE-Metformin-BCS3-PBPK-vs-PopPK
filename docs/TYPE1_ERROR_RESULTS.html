What the comparison shows

The like-for-like comparison is a null result. At its own simulated BE boundary each method returns 4.8–5.4% across every endpoint and both study sizes — as it must, since TOST is calibrated at the limit regardless of variance. The scaled-reference control returns 5.15% (n=48) and 5.39% (n=86).

The divergence is entirely in boundary placement. Plotted against true GMR the two operating characteristics lie on top of one another. But a 1.1% error in the declared T50 boundary moves the true type I error from 5% to 9.6% — an 87% relative error from a one-percent CQA error. This is Morris Part 2's warning, reproduced.

One endpoint fails outright. PBPK's AUCinf boundary hits the upper limit (1.25) while PopPK's hits the lower (0.80), so the two are not comparable at all — independent support for treating AUCinf cautiously.

The operating characteristics are indistinguishable in GMR space

This is the structural finding. As a function of true GMR, PBPK and PopPK produce the same acceptance curve — both cross 5% exactly at 0.80 and 1.25. The two methods do not differ in their test or in their variance; they differ only in the CQA → GMR mapping that decides which T50 sits at the limit.

HOW TO READ

What is actually happening at the boundary

Two different columns

In the Step 3 table the 8.1 and 9.6 are percentages — a true type I error. The 1.66× and 1.85× are the ratios, i.e. 8.1 ÷ 4.9. They are easy to conflate; they answer different questions.

The boundary is a knife-edge tablet

Slower release means a lower Cmax, so as T50 rises the GMR falls. Somewhere there is a T50 where the true GMR is exactly 0.80 — sitting dead on the BE limit. That T50 is the boundary; PBPK puts it at 4.623 h. A tablet made to that release rate is neither quite failing nor quite passing.

The 5% appears there on its own — it is not a result

Type I error is the chance a study concludes “bioequivalent” when the truth sits exactly on the limit. For TOST that is 5% by construction: at a true GMR of 0.80 you pass only if the lower confidence bound climbs above 0.80, which needs the estimate to land about 1.68 standard errors above the truth. A 1-in-20 fluke — no matter how variable the drug, no matter how large the study.

So when both methods return 4.8–5.4% at their own boundary, that agreement is arithmetic, not validation. Both would return 5% even if one of the models were badly wrong. It is the reason the like-for-like comparison had to be a null result.

So what are the 8.1% and 9.6%?

PopPK draws its line at T50 = 4.574 h and reports “type I error here is 4.9%”. But the PBPK truth says that at 4.574 h the real GMR is 0.8049, not 0.8000 — a hair inside the acceptance range, so a tablet there is truthfully just barely bioequivalent.

The real chance a study at that T50 passes is therefore not 5% but 8.1% at n = 48 and 9.6% at n = 86. That is what those numbers are: the actual pass rate of a tablet sitting exactly where PopPK drew its line. PopPK reported 4.9%.

Note what the risk is and is not. The product there is genuinely bioequivalent, so nothing unsafe is waved through. What is wrong is the reported operating characteristic: you would hand a regulator “5%” for a limit that really carries 8–10%.

Why a bigger study makes it worse

Picture the acceptance curve as a cliff face. It is pinned at 5% exactly at the limit for every study size — more subjects cannot help you there, you still need the 1-in-20 fluke. What more subjects do is make the cliff steeper, because the confidence interval narrows.

The anchor does not move, but the wall beside it gets steeper. Take one small step inland from the edge — the 0.005 GMR offset — and a steeper cliff carries you higher up. With more subjects, “genuinely just inside the limit” becomes “reliably demonstrable”.

The takeaway

A bigger, more expensive study offers no protection against a misplaced boundary — it makes the consequence worse. So the accuracy of the CQA → GMR inversion matters more as studies get larger, not less. That is the argument for letting the mechanistic model place the limit even in the case where the sparse-data model reproduces the BE decision correctly — which is exactly what the safe-space comparison found it does.

Three things worth carrying into the write-up

  • A larger study is more fragile, not less. Going from n = 48 to n = 86 raises the cost of the same boundary error (8.1% → 9.6% on per-arm Cmax). Tighter confidence intervals steepen the OC curve, so sample size cannot buy robustness against a misplaced limit.
  • The direction of the error does not replicate. On the upper side the per-arm fit places PopPK's boundary inside PBPK's (inflation, 1.66×) while the joint fit places it outside (deflation, 0.44×). Only the magnitude of the boundary error survives a change of fitting strategy — so report magnitude, and treat sign as fit-dependent.
  • The fast-release side is consistently permissive. Both fits put PopPK's lower boundary below PBPK's — 1.359 h and 1.250 h against 1.457 h, i.e. 7% and 14% too low — where the true GMR is 1.27–1.29 and the product is genuinely not bioequivalent. The practical consequence is a failed study rather than an unsafe product: true acceptance there is only 0.2–1.4%.
LIMITS

What this analysis cannot say

The finding reduces to the boundary error

Because the operating characteristics coincide in GMR space, “T1 true” is fully determined by the true GMR at the declared boundary. The amplification is real and worth reporting, but it is not evidence independent of the boundary discrepancy itself — it is that discrepancy expressed in units a regulator reads.

PBPK is the truth by construction

The PopPK arm is fit to data simulated from the PBPK model, so this tests information loss from sparse sampling and structural mis-specification. It does not test whether the PBPK model itself is right.

The within-subject CVs are healthy-volunteer values

Cmax 12.2% and AUC 11.2% come from the FDA NDA 212595 clinical-pharmacology review. Every acceptance rate on this page scales with those numbers; they are not estimated from this study.

The joint calibration control is not a nominal

Scaling both Cmax and AUC to exactly 0.80 at once returns 0.26% (n=48) and 0.23% (n=86). That is an artifact of putting both endpoints on the limit simultaneously, which does not happen in the T50 sweep — Cmax binds first. Read the joint rows in Step 2 instead.
